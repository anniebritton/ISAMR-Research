Coding Agents in an Environmental Research Pipeline: A Case Study of Spring Green-Up in Wapusk National Park, Canada, 2001–2024

This study examined what coding agents contributed to a complete remote sensing analysis, in which agents built the pipeline, optimised one of its methods in a sandboxed loop, and audited the plan, the literature, and the draft.

Annie Britton Code and data Full report Pre-registration

Summary

Coding agents are increasingly used to build, improve, and review scientific analyses, yet there is little evidence on what they contribute in each role or on the problems they introduce. This study examined that question through a complete analysis of spring green-up in Wapusk National Park, on Hudson Bay, in which a builder agent wrote the pipeline, an optimizer agent tried to improve the green-up method in a sandboxed loop, and auditing agents reviewed the plan, checked the literature, and refereed the draft. The study asked what agents contributed in each role (RQ1), whether the optimizer's improvements carried over to the scientific result (RQ2), and how the optimizer behaved under a fixed objective (RQ3).

In this case, agents added more value by checking the research than by optimising it. The auditors caught 20 of the 24 consequential problems recorded in the project, including an objective that would have biased the trend, whereas the optimizer's improvements on synthetic data did not carry over to real pixels. The worked example itself found no detectable trend in green-up between 2001 and 2024, but strong year-to-year associations with snowmelt and spring warmth.

Agent Roles and Contributions (RQ1)

Every agent in the study was an instance of Claude Code working under the author's direction. The builder wrote the pipeline, pre-registration, and drafts; the optimizer could edit only the file containing the green-up method; the auditors were read-only, with web search for the literature and referee roles; and scripted harness tests deliberately broke the loop's rules to check that its enforcement worked. Consequential problems raised during the project, and the changes they caused, were recorded by role.

The plan reviewer raised the most consequential problem before any code ran, as the first design of the optimizer's objective would have pulled each year toward the long-term average and copied the comparison product's trend. After the first draft, the referee found that the variance partition was misreported, that causal language contradicted the pre-registration, and that an apparent conflict with earlier work rested on a mismatched comparison. The builder introduced several of these errors itself, which means that verification, rather than generation, was the main cost of working with agents.

The Optimizer Agent

Held-Out Performance

The agent's final version modestly improved the method on data it never saw, reducing error on the visible synthetic benchmark from 3.18 to 2.96 days and on the held-out benchmark from 4.65 to 4.39 days, and outperforming all three pre-registered extractors. On held-out seasons built from real NDVI shapes, however, every method erred by more than 8 days against 2–4 days on synthetic seasons, which suggests that the benchmark, rather than the search, was the binding constraint.

Transfer to Real Data (RQ2)

This improvement did not, however, carry over to the year-to-year signal on which Part I depends. On real pixels the final version agreed more closely with MCD12Q2 pixel by pixel, but its park-wide year-to-year agreement fell from 0.900 to 0.881, so it failed two of the four pre-registered entry criteria. An ablation located the trade-off: the snow-flag fix in iteration 8 improved held-out error without losing year-to-year fidelity, whereas the amplitude change in iteration 16 traded that fidelity for lower pixel bias, and a keep-if-better rule on one score could not see the difference. None of the agent's versions moved the conclusions of Part I.

Optimizer Behaviour (RQ3)

The agent searched in a recognisable sequence. It explored five kinds of change in its first seven iterations without success, then found two gains and spent most of the next ten iterations refining one of them; overall, 4 of its 24 substantive attempts were kept. Once substantive ideas stopped succeeding, it turned to deleting lines to shave the line-count penalty, describing these openly as cleanups, and in its last five iterations it reported that no untried idea remained and made no edit. It never attempted to edit another file or to read the held-out data, but it did reason directly from the benchmark generator it was allowed to read, which 26 of its 33 rationales cite.

Lessons for Optimizer Loops

The run suggests three lessons for agents in research pipelines. First, evaluators should score the statistic the science depends on, and keep every version that is not beaten on all objectives rather than accepting changes on one number. Next, a single score was gamed even by an agent that followed every rule, and the gaming appeared exactly when its ideas ran out, so rejecting changes that leave outputs unchanged would remove the incentive while preserving the signal. Lastly, keeping the agent off the inference path meant that none of its changes could move the reported result, and none did. The full account is in LESSONS.md.

What Went Wrong

  1. The optimizer could read the benchmark generator, and 26 of its 33 rationales reasoned from it, which may explain why its gains on real-shaped seasons were small.
  2. The line-count penalty and the fixed penalty for failed seasons invited gaming, and the keep-if-better rule on one score discarded the version that served the science best.
  3. The code of reverted candidates was not saved, so the loop's exploration cannot be re-analysed, and without a stopping rule about USD 10.40 of the USD 17.90 was spent after its real gains ended.
  4. The optimizer could not run code, which kept its hit rate at 17%, and the loop was run once with one model.
  5. The literature audit ran after the confirmatory analysis rather than before it, so several corrections had to be reported as deviations.
  6. The pre-registration was committed four seconds before the confirmatory run, within one session, so its protection rests on the order of operations rather than on elapsed time.
  7. The builder introduced errors that only later audits caught, and the same system coded the optimizer's iterations.

Challenges for Agentic Research

These errors point to five challenges for anyone using agents in research. First, the evaluator defines what the agent improves, and for most scientific methods the quantity that matters, such as a trend or a driver effect, is not the quantity that is easy to score. Second, ground truth is scarce, so benchmarks tend to be synthetic, and an agent that can read how they are built will learn the benchmark rather than the world. Next, agents work faster than the safeguards designed for human researchers, so pre-registration and sequencing must be enforced in code rather than trusted to time. Furthermore, the builder's errors were confident and plausible, so verification becomes the main cost. Lastly, agent runs are hard to reproduce, as model versions change and outputs are not deterministic, so the ledger, the prompts, and every candidate need to be archived.

What to Try Next

The most informative next experiments would change one element of the design at a time. First, the evaluator could score the scientific estimand directly, through synthetic multi-year panels with a known trend and driver effect, while keeping every version that is not beaten on all objectives. Second, the same loop could be run with the benchmark generator readable and unreadable, to measure how much of the gain comes from reading it. Next, the benchmark could be built from real ground truth, such as PhenoCam or field phenology sites across the boreal and tundra zones, with whole sites held out.

The study area could also be varied, as running the same loop across several parks and holding out entire parks would test whether an agent-designed method transfers across landscapes. Letting the agent select its own data sources or predictors is the most tempting extension, but it carries the greatest risk, since choosing data after seeing results is the agentic equivalent of searching for significance; it should be tried only if the agent proposes its choices before seeing the outcome, a human approves them, and the confirmatory test runs on held-out years or parks. Smaller changes, such as a sandboxed tool for testing changes, saved candidates, a stopping rule, and comparisons across models, would also be informative, whereas longer runs, more powerful search on a synthetic benchmark, and any role for agents in the confirmatory test appear less promising. Lastly, giving auditing and optimising agents equal budgets across several studies would test whether checking consistently adds more than optimising.

The Worked Example: Green-Up in Wapusk

The analysis the agents worked on estimated mid-green-up, the day on which smoothed MODIS NDVI reaches half of its seasonal amplitude, for 300 land pixels between 2001 and 2024, under an analysis plan fixed before any trend or relationship was examined. It tested three alternative hypotheses: that green-up advanced (H1), that later snow-off brings later green-up (H2), and that spring warmth explains variation beyond snow-off (H3). The results do not support H1 but support H2 and H3.

Study Area

Wapusk covers 11,475 km² of peatland, fen, coastal tundra, and open spruce forest on the western shore of Hudson Bay, where polar bears summer on land and caribou and nesting geese depend on the timing of fresh forage. The sample drew 75 pixels at random from each of four land-cover strata, excluding pixels with more than 10% open water, so the results describe the drier ~70% of the park.

Snow-Off and Spring Warmth (H2, H3)

Snow-off and warmth overlap substantially, as 43% of the explained variance is shared between them, which is consistent with a single chain in which warm springs bring early melt and early green-up. The contribution of warmth beyond snow-off, however, depended on how the temperature window was drawn, falling to an increase in R² of 0.09 when the window ended on 15 May rather than 31 May.

Sensitivity Analysis

Every result was re-estimated under 24 combinations of smoother, threshold, cloud mask, and aggregation, and again under one-at-a-time changes to each parameter that the literature does not fix. No variant produced a trend whose interval excluded zero, and snow-off remained significant throughout; the precision of the trend estimate, however, is specific to the primary specification.

Season Explorer

Each green-up date comes from a single pixel's season of cloud-gapped daily NDVI. Snow-flagged days anchor the curve at the dormant baseline, and the smoothed curve's crossing of half the seasonal amplitude gives the date.

Applications

Park Managers and Ecologists

The timing of fresh forage in Wapusk appears to be predictable from conditions in a given spring rather than from a long-term trend. Monitoring and planning that depend on forage timing, such as caribou and goose surveys or fieldwork on breeding success, could therefore use snow-off and April–May warmth as early indicators; in 20 of the 24 years both were available by the end of May, about a month before mid-green-up, and together they predicted it to within about four days. The absence of a detectable trend should not be read as stability, however, since advances of the size reported nearby for 2004–2016 cannot be excluded.

Applying the Method Elsewhere

The pipeline is configured rather than hard-coded, so it can be applied to another park by replacing the boundary GeoJSON, the station climate IDs, and the seasonal windows in config.yaml, then running wapusk sample and wapusk all; outside Canada, ERA5-Land degree days can replace the station series. Three design choices are likely to transfer to other high-latitude sites: estimating phenology per pixel on a fixed stratified sample, anchoring snow-covered days at a dormant baseline, and defining green-up relative to the seasonal amplitude rather than the annual maximum.

Building Agent Loops and Agentic Pipelines

The loop in autoresearch/ can be reused for any method that fits in one function with a measurable error. The case suggests seven design rules:

  1. Score the statistic the science depends on, not only per-sample accuracy.
  2. Keep every version that is not beaten on all objectives, and choose among them at the end.
  3. Reject changes whose outputs are identical to the current version, which removes the incentive to game penalties while keeping gaming as a signal that the search is exhausted.
  4. Build benchmarks from real data where possible, and hold out a family derived from real data.
  5. Enforce every rule in code outside the agent, test that code with scripted rule-breaking agents, and keep the agent away from inference.
  6. Hide the benchmark generator from the agent, and save every candidate so the loop doubles as a sensitivity analysis.
  7. Run auditing agents on the plan before any code is written and on the literature before the confirmatory analysis, and record what each one catches.

Methods in Brief

Daily MOD09GA collection 6.1 reflectance was quality masked using the state and quality bits, and NDVI was computed for each pixel between 2001 and 2024. For each pixel, a dormant baseline was set at the 5th percentile of snow-free NDVI outside the growing season; each season was smoothed with a Savitzky–Golay filter fitted to the upper envelope, and mid-green-up was taken as the first day the curve reached half of its amplitude. Snow-off came from park-mean ERA5-Land snow cover and thawing degree days from the Churchill climate station, and trends were estimated with Theil–Sen slopes and Mann–Kendall tests, while associations were estimated with standardized least squares, a nested F-test, and commonality analysis. The peer-reviewed basis for each choice, and the sensitivity test behind each parameter, are documented in the methods justification.

Limitations

  1. Twenty-four years offer limited sensitivity to trends: the pre-registered power analysis put the minimum detectable trend near 0.9 days/yr, and most specifications cannot exclude an advance of 0.56 days/yr.
  2. The driver models rest on 24 points, and the snow-off association weakens without the two most extreme years (2004 and 2009) or when only genuine station records are used.
  3. The predictors are coarse, as ERA5-Land resolves the park at about 9 km and overestimates snow duration, and thawing degree days come from a single coastal station.
  4. The results describe the drier ~70% of the park, and the land-cover map used for weighting classifies wetland poorly.
  5. All comparisons are between MODIS retrievals; no ground phenology was available to validate the dates.
  6. The agent study is a single case, with one research task, one model family, and one optimizer run, so its findings are suggestive rather than general.
  7. The record of contributions by role was compiled by the builder and is not exhaustive, and the coding of the optimizer's iterations was performed by the same system.
